Barrel Rocket Mass Heater

An 8-inch J-tube rocket heater under your 55-gallon drum, venting through a sand-and-pebble sleeping bench. The drum throws heat while the fire burns; the bench gives it back through the night, so the fire can be out before you sleep.

Checks that decide whether and where

These are legal and life-safety calls, so they are yours. Each one shows what the record says and what is still open. Tick them off as you settle them; the ticks are saved in this browser only.

  1. Record Ecology: “It is illegal to install an uncertified wood stove in Washington… a homeowner cannot apply to certify their wood stove.” PSCAA says the same, and its 2012 rule change added “a prohibition on installing unapproved devices.” ORCAA lists “installation… of an uncertified solid fuel burning device by a private citizen” as a violation. No Washington rule mentions rocket mass heaters. The site-built masonry-heater route (WAC 51-51-1002) needs an Ecology-approved type with tight-fitting doors and outside combustion air; this design has an open feed tube.

    Inferred This heater is an uncertified device with no path to certification, so installing it in a building conflicts with those rules. Rests on the four sources above and the absence of any rocket-heater rule.

    Not in record Which county the outbuilding is in. Seattle / King County is PSCAA, (206) 716-1195. Tahuya / Mason County is ORCAA, orcaa.org. That agency is the check.

  2. Record The residential code expects solid-fuel room heaters to be tested to UL 1482 and installed per their listing (IRC M1410.1), so a site-built heater needs the building official’s approval. PSCAA: installing an uncertified stove “can void insurance policy coverage in some instances.” One county building department (Coconino, AZ) reviewed rocket mass heaters and declined to approve them, citing escaping gases.

    Not in record Whether sleeping in this outbuilding needs a permit or dwelling approval (not verified this session). Check with the county building department and your insurer.

  3. Inferred The bench alone weighs about 2,800–3,000 lb: roughly 29 ft³ of sand and pebbles at 95–103 lb/ft³. That is about 140 lb on every square foot it covers, before the core and drum. It goes on a slab on grade, not a framed floor.

    Not in record What is under the spot you picked.

  4. Record The feed tube is open to the room, so if the draft reverses, smoke and CO come in. Builders report CO trouble mostly as the fire is dying down.

    Put a CO alarm and a smoke alarm in the room you sleep in and test them before any fire. The code sections that likely require them (RCW 19.27.530, IRC R315) were named in research but not read this session.

  5. Record OSHA 1910.252(a)(3)(i): no cutting or welding on used drums “until they have been cleaned so thoroughly as to make absolutely certain that there are no flammable materials present.” NIOSH case reports include a sealed drum with leftover diesel that exploded under a cut-off saw.

    Not in record What your drum held, and whether it is galvanized or lined. Don’t cut it until you know, it’s clean, and it’s open to air.

  6. Record The system stays one size from feed tube to chimney cap. Galvanized coatings are rated for 392°F continuous; zinc melts at 787°F (419.5°C), and riser-top readings peak around 1,400°F. Zinc oxide fume causes metal fume fever.

    Not in record Your pipe’s outside diameter and whether it is galvanized. These sheets are drawn for 8-inch.

  7. Sheet S-3 draws these lines.

  8. Record Single-wall pipe must not pass through a wall, ceiling or roof; use a listed insulated chimney with a listed support or firestop. The top goes at least 3 ft above the roof where it passes through and 2 ft higher than anything within 10 ft.

    Inferred If the building is old, the roofing or siding you cut through is a sampling question under your own survey rules.

If check 1 comes back no

The lawful version is a certified wood stove, new or used-and-certified, on a hearth poured from your sand, cement and pebbles, with a masonry bench or wall beside it, outside the stove’s listed clearances, to soak up and hold heat. The drum and loose pipe don’t go in that version, and the chimney is listed parts either way. Inferred

What the plan needs that isn’t on your list

  • Firebrick, standard hard 9 × 4.5 × 2.5 in: about 40 for the floor, tunnel, roof and feed tube, and about 50 more for a firebrick riser shaft; add 10% for cuts. Inferred Counted from these sheets; no authoritative count was found.
  • Clay: bagged fireclay or dug clay subsoil, for slip, mortar, seals and the perlite mix.
  • Perlite (garden center), if your pebbles aren’t perlite. Or ceramic fiber blanket, which is an IARC 2B possible carcinogen and forms cristobalite after firing; handle it like the work you already do.
  • Riser sleeve: 18 in OD, plain sheet steel, not galvanized.
  • Fittings: 8-in black stovepipe for the first run, one 8-in tee with a cap for the chimney base, a plate or cap for the manifold cleanout, sheet-metal screws, high-temperature sealant.
  • Listed chimney parts: ceiling or roof support, insulated chimney sections to height, flashing, storm collar, cap with spark screen.
  • Safety: CO alarm, smoke alarm, ABC extinguisher, lidded metal ash can, P100 respirator for perlite, cement and fiber dust.
  • Optional: a ¼-in steel disc to lay inside the drum lid. The lid is a wear part (reported life over two years) and the disc extends it; measure gap G to the disc. An infrared thermometer to read the exhaust (140–200°F is the target).

Sequence

Build it in this order

  1. Settle the checks.

    Checks 1 and 3 decide whether and where this gets built. Nothing below matters until they are answered.

  2. Measure and sort what you have.

    Pipe outside diameter; galvanized or black; what the drum held; what the “packing pebbles” are.

  3. Make the drum safe, then cut it.

    Clean and vent it first. Cut off the end with the bung holes; the plain end becomes the lid. Burn the paint off outdoors, upwind, until it is bare steel.

  4. Pour the slab.

    Dig out, lay 4 in of compacted gravel, pour 4 in of 1:2:3 concrete, reaching 18 in past the core on every side and under the whole bench. Let it cure before loading it.

  5. Lay the insulating layer.

    2 in of perlite-clay under the core footprint. Let it dry.

  6. Dry-fit the core.

    Floor, tunnel walls, roof and feed tube without mortar. Check A through E against the table and check that the drum clears the feed tube by 2.25 in. Then set the bricks in thin clay slip or fireclay mortar.

  7. Build the riser.

    Shaft to F (42.4 in above the tunnel floor), the 18-in sleeve around it, insulation packed between, and a cap over the top edge of the insulation.

  8. Test the open core.

    With the drum off, burn paper and small sticks: the flame should lay over and run into the riser. If it won’t, fix it now while everything is reachable. Inferred

  9. Build the manifold and set the drum.

    Firebrick and clay, floor below the duct inlet, cleanout 1 on the side. Measure G, set the drum, seal the rim with clay-sand.

  10. Run the duct and the chimney.

    Black steel for the first run, every joint screwed and sealed, the capped tee at the chimney base, listed support and insulated chimney through the roof, spark-screen cap, 3 ft / 2 ft / 10 ft.

  11. Smoke-test before burying anything.

    Run a fire and check every joint and seal. Fix leaks while they are still exposed. Inferred

  12. Build the bench.

    Shell walls, clay slip painted on the duct, fill in tamped layers, clay-sand cap near the manifold, concrete cap beyond.

  13. Dry it out.

    Small, short fires until the masonry is dry. Wet masonry heated fast can crack or spall.

  14. Alarms up, extinguisher by the door.

    Then the first full burn.

Operation

Run it

The sleeping rule

Never sleep with a fire in it. Burn in the evening while you are up: the drum heats the room while it burns, and the bench gives heat back overnight. Builders report bench tops of 100–120°F at the hot end and 80–90°F at the far end on 8-inch systems, and 130–140°F after a full day of burning.

  1. Before lighting.

    Test the CO alarm. Nothing inside the clearance lines, nothing on the drum. Check the agency’s burn-ban status.

  2. Prime a cold system.

    Light a twist of newspaper in the chimney-base tee, cap it, then light the feed tube.

  3. Light.

    Paper and kindling standing in the feed tube. Add split dry wood standing on end once it draws hard.

  4. Feed.

    Keep the tube loaded but not packed. Cut sticks to about the tube’s height; overlong sticks are a reported cause of smoke coming back out.

  5. Fuel.

    Dry, seasoned wood, with paper to start. Washington bans burning garbage, treated or painted wood, plastics, rubber and similar (WAC 173-433-120).

  6. Watch the chimney.

    Visible smoke after start-up means a poor burn. The state limit is 20% opacity for 6 minutes in any hour; start-up gets 20 minutes in any 4 hours.

  7. Finish while awake.

    Stop feeding and let it burn down to ash. Builders report CO trouble mostly as the fire dies down, so be up for that part.

  8. Close the feed.

    Once no coals glow, set a lid on the feed tube so the chimney doesn’t pull the bench’s heat out overnight. Inferred

  9. Ash.

    Shovel it out of the tunnel cold, into a lidded metal can, outside.

Burn bans

In a Stage 1 ban, PSCAA and ORCAA don’t allow uncertified devices unless the device is your only adequate source of heat and you hold an approved exemption (PSCAA) or have notified the agency (ORCAA). In Stage 2, no wood burning at all, with the same exception. PSCAA’s last exemption approvals ran until August 31, 2026. “Adequate source of heat” means holding 70°F at 3 ft above the floor in all normally inhabited areas. Record
